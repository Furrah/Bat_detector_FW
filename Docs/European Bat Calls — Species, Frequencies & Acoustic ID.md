# European Bat Calls — Species, Frequencies & Acoustic ID

Oct 29, 2026 · @Joe

## Short answer

Frequency alone reliably separates only a minority of European bats. It works for the horseshoe bats, which each hold a narrow constant-frequency band, for the audible free-tailed bat, and usually for the three common pipistrelles.

Elsewhere the bands overlap heavily. A dozen or so *Myotis* species share broadband sweeps with peaks in roughly the same range, and noctules, serotines and the parti-coloured bat all crowd into 20–30 kHz.

What works better is a combination of features: call shape, the frequency where the call ends, duration and pulse interval, the rhythm across a sequence, social calls, and habitat and flight behaviour. Even then, several groups (most *Myotis* pairs, the long-eared bats) usually stop at genus level from sound alone.

The tables below cover all 46 species of geographic Europe and its islands, grouped by call type. [EUROBATS](https://eurobats.org/about_eurobats/protected_bat_species) lists 55 across its wider agreement area, which reaches into North Africa and the Middle East.

Pipistrelles and *Myotis* interleave across 35–57 kHz, so a peak value there could belong to a dozen species. Even with four call parameters, most Bechstein's, whiskered, Brandt's and Leisler's calls were misclassified.

## How a call is described

Every call is read from its shape on a spectrogram plus a few measured parameters. The shape sorts a bat into a group; the parameters narrow it down within that group.

| Call shape | What it looks like | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CF-FM | Long pure tone (tens of ms) ending in a short downward sweep | Horseshoe bats |
| FM-QCF | Steep sweep into a flat tail, the "hockey stick" | Pipistrelles, Savi's pipistrelle, Schreiber's bat; noctules and serotines near clutter |
| QCF | Long, almost flat, low tone | Noctules, serotines and parti-coloured bat in open air; free-tailed bat |
| Steep broadband FM | Fast sweep of 1–8 ms with no flat tail | All *Myotis* |
| Faint multi-harmonic FM | Quiet sweep with a strong second harmonic | Long-eared bats (*Plecotus*) |
| Two alternating types | Short loud sweep, then a longer higher one | Barbastelle |
| Clicks | Paired, very short broadband tongue clicks | Egyptian fruit bat |

| Parameter | Meaning | Most useful for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peak frequency (FmaxE) | Frequency carrying the most energy | Horseshoe and FM-QCF bats |
| End / characteristic frequency | Lowest point of the call, or the flat tail's frequency | Pipistrelles; *Myotis* end frequency |
| Start frequency, bandwidth | Highest frequency; start minus end | *Myotis* (Natterer's can sweep over 100 kHz in about 1 ms) |
| Duration | Length of one call, ms | *Myotis*, noctules vs serotines |
| Inter-pulse interval | Time between calls; sets the rhythm | Alternation, regular vs irregular rhythm |
| Shape details | Knee, heel, curvature, explosive or soft onset | Separating *Myotis* species |

The same bat changes its calls with its surroundings. Near vegetation calls get shorter, broader and higher; in open air they get longer, flatter and lower ([Obrist et al. 2004](https://core.ac.uk/download/85210161.pdf)). So compare search-phase calls from similar situations, and judge whole sequences rather than single calls ([Bas et al. 2011](https://www.batsurvey.org/wp-content/uploads/VisualDeterminationOfMyotis.pdf)).

## Horseshoe bats: one number nearly settles it

All five European horseshoe bats hold a long constant-frequency (CF) tone, tens of milliseconds long, in a narrow species-specific band. Only the three highest-calling species overlap with each other.

The CF value shifts a little with sex, age, body size and region, and flying bats lower their emitted tone to compensate for Doppler shift. So treat each band as ±2–3 kHz soft at the edges. Switzerland has only the greater and lesser horseshoe, and the Pays de Gex next door is very likely the same, so for you CF alone is decisive.

## *Myotis*: where frequency fails

Fifteen European *Myotis* species sweep through much the same 20–110 kHz range in a few milliseconds. In the Swiss study, four call parameters classified 59% of *Myotis* calls correctly against 84% for other bats ([Obrist 2004](https://core.ac.uk/download/85210161.pdf)). ## A step-by-step acoustic key

One look at the shape removes most candidates; frequency then decides cleanly only within the horseshoe and pipistrelle groups. Anything still ambiguous goes to social calls, then range, then a group-level record.

## What works better than frequency

In order of usefulness: call shape, then the frequency where the call ends or flattens, then rhythm across a sequence, then social calls, then habitat and range. Automated classifiers combine these and still struggle with *Myotis*.

- **Shape first.** It sorts a call into its group before any number is read; frequency then works inside the group.
- **End or tail frequency, not only peak.** A pipistrelle's flat tail is stable. For *Myotis*, the end frequency and how it moves as calls lengthen separate species better than the peak ([Bas et al. 2011](https://www.batsurvey.org/wp-content/uploads/VisualDeterminationOfMyotis.pdf)).
- **Rhythm and alternation.** Noctules and barbastelles alternate two call types, the northern bat phrases in threes, and mouse-eared bats are strikingly regular ([Barataud key](https://plecotus.natagora.be/fileadmin/Pole_Plecotus/biblio/BarataudM_Cle_expansion.pdf)).
- **Sequences, not single calls.** Single *Myotis* calls are rarely identifiable; a sequence containing several call types lets you eliminate candidates until one remains.
- **Social calls.** They settle Kuhl's vs Nathusius', the pipistrelles vs Schreiber's, and the Crete pipistrelle. Most sit around 14–22 kHz, well below the echolocation calls.
- **Behaviour and context.** Low trawling over water with rippled "dotted" calls points to the Daubenton's group; a bat working inside foliage points to gleaners.
- **Range.** Several pairs are separated in practice only by where you are.
- **Classifiers.** Discriminant analysis on four call parameters scored about 82% for 18 Italian species ([Russo & Jones 2002](https://peeref.com/works/72666883)) and 75% for 26 Swiss species. A neural-network tool covering 34 European species reached a median of 83.7% ([Walters et al. 2012](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-continentalscale-tool-for-acoustic-identification/99576108902621)). *Myotis* score lowest everywhere, so treat output as a probability and check it by eye.

## What this means for your STM32G431 detector

The 500 ksps front end covers every European bat, but FFT-peak auto-tuning will only name species reliably for horseshoe bats and the pipistrelle group. Everything else needs per-call features measured across a sequence.

- **Microphone band.** Nyquist is 250 kHz, comfortably above the highest *Myotis* starts (\~140 kHz). The SPVA1A0LR5H-1 is rated to about 80 kHz, so lesser, Mediterranean and Mehely's horseshoes (102–112 kHz) sit above its rated band — exactly the bats where one number identifies the species. Measure its real response to 120 kHz before settling the response-shaping question.
- **FFT size.** 1024 points at 500 ksps gives 488 Hz bins over a 2.05 ms frame. That suits CF tones and QCF tails. *Myotis* calls last 1–5 ms, so one frame smears the whole sweep; a 256-point frame (0.51 ms, 1.95 kHz bins) with 75% overlap resolves the shape.
- **Auto-tuning on the peak.** Works well for CF and FM-QCF calls, which concentrate energy in one narrow band. For noctules and barbastelles the peak flips between two values, and for *Myotis* it wanders with clutter. Take the median over several calls, or tune to the call's end frequency.
- **Per-call features.** Segment calls with an energy threshold, then log start, end and peak frequency, duration, interval to the previous call and an alternation flag. A rule set — shape group first, then frequency band — fits easily on the Cortex-M4F and can report a group ("*Myotis* sp.") when a species is not safe.
- **Social calls.** Pipistrelle, Kuhl's and Schreiber's social calls sit around 14–22 kHz, well inside the mic's band. Make sure the trigger does not reject low-frequency events, because they are often the deciding evidence.
- **Recordings for proper ID.** Keep whole sequences (the PSRAM pre-trigger already helps) and write WAVs with the true 500 kHz sample rate, so desktop classifiers read real frequencies. Adding the GUANO metadata chunk that many bat tools read would round off the WAV-header task.
- **Local reference set.** The Swiss dataset in the chart above is the closest published reference to the Pays de Gex, and a sensible starting set for testing any on-device rules.
